### P4 — Premium pricing model (DeepSeek, 4077–4231)
**What it does that our own `dfw-pricing-v2.md` doesn't:** DeepSeek is the only source in this entire research program that shows the **full arithmetic** for a delivery-rate card by truck class (small ≤8yd / tandem 12yd-15t / end-dump 20yd-24t) × distance ring (0–10/10–20/20–35mi), then walks a 10-unit order through wholesale-cost → premium-tier price → gross margin for all three premium levels. This is a genuinely useful **method**, not just a number.

**Consensus with our research:** DeepSeek's recommended default (+35% over local-retail-median + delivery, with +25% for commodity/price-shopped SKUs and +50% for specialty/urgent) lands in the same neighborhood as `dfw-pricing-v2.md`'s decision to keep `dallasFortWorth.ts`'s `premiumRate` at 0.35 unchanged. Independent convergence on "+35%" from two different methods (DeepSeek's SKU-by-SKU model vs. our own scraper-derived median) is a reasonably strong signal that 35% is a defensible default, not just an arbitrary round number.

**Disagreement / weakness:** DeepSeek's wholesale assumption ("80% of local retail median" for all SKUs, ranging 75–85% by category) is **stated as an assumption carried over from the prompt's own instructions**, not independently sourced — it cannot be verified from public data (wholesale-to-yard pricing isn't published anywhere) and should be treated as a modeling convenience, not a fact. Its retail-median inputs are pulled from the same P1 CSV `external-llm-competitor-synthesis.md` and `dfw-pricing-v2.md` already flag as mixing yard and broker price classes — DeepSeek doesn't re-split those classes, so a few of its "retail median" inputs (e.g., decomposed granite at $43.02/yd) likely understate true local-yard retail versus `dfw-pricing-v2.md`'s more careful class-split analysis. **Use DeepSeek's method (rate-card-by-ring, 10-unit math walkthrough), not its specific dollar inputs** — those should be re-run against `dfw-pricing-v2.md`'s already-cleaned class-split data.

**Genuinely new idea:** the **A/B test design** (ZIP-cluster cells matched on income/distance/competitor-density/historical AOV, 100–200 quotes per cell before reading results, explicit decision rules for moving a SKU up or down a premium tier) is more rigorous than anything in our own research and is directly actionable — worth lifting into `dfw-pricing-v2.md` or a future pricing-experimentation doc as a named next step.

### P6 — 90-day low-cost GTM (4 independent answers: DeepSeek 5695, Gemini 6250, unlabeled/ChatGPT-style 7043, unlabeled/Grok-style 7302)
See §5 for the merged plan. Key cross-engine findings:

**Strong consensus (all 4 engines independently rank in their top 5):** Google Business Profile (SAB), yard signs on delivered piles, referral credits via SMS, review-generation SMS, door hangers in new-construction subdivisions. This is the same top tier `dfw-low-cost-gtm-and-tiktok.md` already identified — strong independent confirmation, not a new finding, but useful validation.

**Disagreement #1 — Google Local Services Ads.** Two of four external engines (Gemini, and the unlabeled ChatGPT-style answer) put **$1,200 straight into LSA** assuming it will simply work under "Landscaping" or "Landscaping/Hauling," with no eligibility-check gate. DeepSeek is the only one that flags real uncertainty ("Delivery/courier is not in the core eligible category list... If 'Moving' category is available, apply under that") and suggests the **Moving-category workaround** as a genuinely new idea. **Verification (this pass):** confirmed **[FACT]** that Moving is a documented LSA-eligible category and that delivery/courier/bulk-materials-supply is **not** documented as eligible as of this pass. The Moving-category workaround is **plausible but not verified to actually pass Google's screening for a materials-delivery business that isn't really a moving company** — this could constitute a category-integrity risk (Google increasingly verifies the actual service performed, and a false "Moving" registration risks suspension). **This is a new idea worth testing cheaply (the eligibility checker itself is free and instant), but should not be assumed to work, and should not be the basis for committing $800–$1,200 of the $5,000 budget before Week 1's actual eligibility-checker result comes back** — this directly reinforces `dfw-low-cost-gtm-and-tiktok.md`'s existing "genuinely uncertain, check first" framing over Gemini/ChatGPT's confident $1,200 commitment.

**Disagreement #2 — GBP primary category.** Covered in P3 above; DeepSeek's "Delivery service" framing (repeated in its P6 tactic #3) is the outlier against Gemini's P3 and P6 answers, which both use materials-supplier categories. Recommend materials-supplier framing (see P3 section).

**Disagreement #3 — AOV assumption, and why it matters.** DeepSeek and Gemini's P6 answers both assume **AOV ≈ $85/order** — this is wildly inconsistent with our own real MGG order data (`docs/metro/HANDOFF-LOG.md`: "$1,473 AOV" nationally, "TX 6 orders at $1,055") and with the prompt's own stated context ("Typical order: 5–20 cubic yards or 5–25 tons, $300–$1,500" — verbatim from the context block every engine was given). An $85 AOV assumption is off by roughly **12–17×** versus the business's actual numbers, and it silently deflates every "CAC ceiling" and ROI calculation built on top of it in both of those plans. The unlabeled ChatGPT-style answer (7043–7300) is the only one of the four that used a plausible **~$250–400 AOV** assumption (still below MGG's real historical AOV, but far closer than $85) — **its CAC/order-count math should be trusted over DeepSeek's or Gemini's, and any budget allocation drawn from the $85-AOV plans should be re-derived against MGG's real $1,000+ AOV before use.** This is the single most decision-relevant numerical error found in the P6 corpus — a low AOV assumption makes CAC-per-order targets look artificially tight and could push toward under-investing in higher-cost, higher-intent channels (like LSA or partnerships) that are actually easily affordable at MGG's real order economics.

**Genuinely new tactics vs. `dfw-low-cost-gtm-and-tiktok.md`:**
1. **Home Depot / Lowe's parking-lot flyering to contractors loading bagged material** (proposed independently by 3 of 4 P6 engines) — not in our existing doc at all. Low cost ($50–100), explicitly flagged by the engines themselves as higher-friction/possible-store-pushback; worth a cautious, low-volume test, not a planned tactic.
2. **10DLC Twilio registration as an explicit line item** (DeepSeek) — a real compliance step (A2P 10DLC registration is required for US business SMS at any volume) that our existing GTM doc's Twilio-based tactics (review SMS, referral SMS, SMS keyword) assume but never names as its own action item. **This should be added to the Week-1 checklist regardless of which GTM plan is followed** — it's a real prerequisite, not a nice-to-have.
3. **HOA/community-newsletter placement timed to spring/fall cleanup weeks** (Gemini) — more specific than our existing doc's general HOA-partnership tactic; the seasonal-timing angle is new and low-cost to test.
4. **New-construction targeting via city permit portals / certificate-of-occupancy timing** (DeepSeek, unlabeled-ChatGPT) — more operationally specific than our existing doc's general "target the fastest-growing suburbs" framing; using permit data to find *actively-building* subdivisions (not just fast-growing cities) is a sharper targeting method worth adopting.
